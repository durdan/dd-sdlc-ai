Uber rides: from request to live trip

How a ride goes from request to live trip: an upfront fare, drivers found by H3 hexagon and ranked by road ETA, an offer loop, and a location stream that keeps both maps moving.

1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

Uber's rides platform connects two kinds of people: riders who want a trip and drivers who are nearby and willing to take it. In the fourth quarter of 2024 Uber reported about 33 million trips a day across mobility and delivery, for 171 million monthly active platform consumers.

Everything in the design depends on one idea: knowing, every few seconds, where every available driver is, and turning that into a match within seconds of a request. Location comes in as a constant stream from driver phones; it is indexed by H3 hexagon so dispatch can find nearby drivers cheaply, and ranked by road ETA so "nearby" means close by road, not on the map.

This walkthrough looks at the system from the outside, using Uber's public results, engineering posts and talks. Where public sources stop, the design is ours and is labelled as such.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, from Uber's own results: about 33 million trips a day in Q4 2024 (mobility and delivery together), 171 million monthly active platform consumers, and $44.0 billion of revenue for the full year 2024. Quarterly trips in 2024 were 2.6, 2.8, 2.9 and 3.1 billion: over 11 billion in the year.

Back of the envelope: 33 million ÷ 86,400 seconds ≈ 380 trips starting every second on average; peaks are several times that. Each active driver also sends a location update every few seconds, so the location stream is far busier than the request path.

The functional requirements below are ours, written from the product rather than from an Uber document: an upfront fare before requesting; a nearby driver matched within seconds; both sides see each other move, live; nobody is charged twice or charged a fare they were not quoted; and a failure somewhere never strands a trip already under way.

Figures often repeated elsewhere, such as a million requests a second, 99.99% uptime or sub-100 ms latency, have no public Uber source we could find, so they are not used here.

3 · Context

Who and what the system talks to (C4 level 1).

Two kinds of user. Riders ask for a fare, request, track the driver and pay. Drivers stream their location, accept offers and complete trips. The driver side is a constant real-time stream, not occasional requests, which is why the diagram separates request and response (solid) from real-time push (dashed).

The platform leans on outside services:

  • Maps and routing data. Uber runs its own mapping, and its 2023 Google Cloud agreement included Google Maps Platform.
  • Payment networks for card and wallet charges and driver payouts. Uber does not publish its providers; the box is illustrative.
  • Push and SMS (for example APNs and FCM) for offers and trip updates. Also illustrative: our assumption for a mobile app, not a published list.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Inside dispatch. Uber has described its dispatch system (in a 2015 talk) and H3 (2018), but not every internal component, so this level is partly our design; the matcher and the offer loop are illustrative. In 2015 dispatch work was spread across nodes by area using Ringpop, a consistent-hashing library; that may not reflect today's system.

  • Demand intake. The pickup point is turned into an H3 cell.
  • Candidate search. Reads the drivers in that cell and the rings of cells around it (a k-ring) from the supply index. This is cheap because every hexagon's neighbours are the same distance away, so rings grow evenly.
  • ETA ranker. Candidates are ranked by road ETA from the routing engine, not by straight-line distance: a driver across a river can be close on the map and far by road.
  • Matcher (illustrative). Picks the pairing. Uber has not published its current matching algorithm.
  • Offer manager (illustrative). Sends the trip to one driver and waits. If the driver declines or the offer times out, the loop returns to the matcher for the next best driver.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

One ride request, start to finish. The ordering is our reconstruction.

  1. The rider picks a product and asks for a fare; Pricing returns an upfront fare. Uber's own description says surge is set per area when requests outpace available drivers.
  2. The rider confirms; the gateway passes the request to Dispatch.
  3. Dispatch looks up drivers in the nearby H3 cells and asks the ETA service for a road ETA per candidate.
  4. The best driver gets an offer. On accept, a trip is created and the rider is told who is coming and when.

Live trip: location in, map out.

  1. The driver app keeps sending GPS points. Third-party write-ups often quote every four seconds; Uber has not published the interval, so treat that as an assumption.
  2. Location ingest validates each point and snaps it to the road, then publishes a location event to Kafka.
  3. Consumers update the supply index (the driver moves to a new cell) and the trip (the on-trip position). The rider's map and ETA are pushed, not polled.
  4. On completion the trip publishes a completed event. Billing, receipts and analytics all start from that one event.

7 · Data and caching

What is stored where, for how long, and what is never stored.

Three kinds of data, three kinds of store.

DataWhereHow long
Trips, accounts, other transactional recordsDocstore, Uber's database built on MySQL (evolved from Schemaless)Kept
Things that happened: trip completed, driver movedEvents on Kafka, read independently by many consumersRetention window

Long-running steps such as charging at trip endCadence workflow state (our illustration)Until the workflow finishes

One rule ties the stores together in our design: a completed trip is handed on as an event, not as a query. Billing, receipts and analytics each read the trip-completed event from Kafka; none of them reads the trip database directly. That keeps the trip store small and fast, and a new consumer can be added without touching it. Uber describes Kafka as its event backbone, but this exact split is ours, not a published diagram.

Sharded MySQL via Vitess is often attributed to Uber; we found no source for it. Uber documents Docstore instead.

8 · Growth

How the same design grows from launch to its current scale.

How it grew, from public sources. The shape stayed; what changed is how many of each part there are, and where they run.

  • 2015. Uber described its dispatch system publicly: supply indexed by location, candidates ranked by ETA, and work spread across nodes with consistent hashing (Ringpop).
  • 2017. Uber open-sourced Cadence, its workflow engine. (Temporal is a separate 2019 fork by Cadence's co-creators, not Cadence renamed.)
  • 2018. H3, the hexagonal hierarchical grid built for pricing and dispatch, was open-sourced.
  • 2023. Seven-year deals to move most on-premises data-centre workloads to Oracle Cloud and Google Cloud, including Google Maps Platform.
  • 2024. Stateless services and batch workloads finished moving from Mesos and Peloton to Kubernetes.

9 · Failure modes

What breaks, what users see, and what protects them.

Uber does not publish its failure handling, so this chapter is our design, labelled as such in the video.

  • ETA service slow. Rank by straight-line distance with a cap and show the ETA as approximate: a circuit breaker with a fallback, with a timeout on each call.
  • Driver ignores the offer. Time out and offer the next candidate; after a few, tell the rider.
  • Pricing unavailable. Never accept a request without a quote; ask the rider to retry.
  • Payment down at trip end. A durable workflow keeps retrying, with an idempotency key so the rider is never charged twice; the trip still completes.
  • Location stream lags. Ignore stale positions and widen the search ring.
  • Driver loses signal in a tunnel. This happens all day. The trip keeps the last known position and the rider's map shows it as approximate rather than frozen. The phone keeps recording points and uploads what it buffered on reconnect, so the trip catches up and the fare reflects the real route.

10 · Patterns used

The named patterns, each linked to its pattern walkthrough.

  • Geospatial index: H3 hexagons for supply and pricing.
  • Event streaming: a completed trip is published once on Kafka, and billing, receipts and analytics each read it.
  • Durable workflows: steps that must finish even if a machine dies (Cadence).
  • Consistent hashing: spreading dispatch work across nodes (Ringpop, as described in 2015); dispatch is effectively sharded by area.
  • Circuit breaker with fallback: around ETA and payments (our design).
  • Transactional outbox: in our MVP, so trip events are never lost between the database and the stream.

Patterns used

  • Geospatial index: H3 hexagons for supply and pricing
  • Event streaming: publish once, many consumers (Kafka)
  • Durable workflows: steps that must finish (Cadence)
  • Consistent hashing: spread dispatch work across nodes (Ringpop, 2015)

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a small ride-hailing platform, not Uber's:

  • One modular service, not thousands; split it later when load or team size demands it.
  • Postgres for accounts, quotes and trips, with trips as a strict state machine.
  • H3 cells and an in-memory supply index for live drivers.
  • Upfront quotes valid for two minutes; never dispatch without one.
  • Greedy match by road ETA with a 15-second offer timeout.
  • A transactional outbox, so events are never lost: a simple stream now, Kafka later.

A word on accuracy. The case study this started from had dated numbers, such as 118 million users and $31.8 billion of revenue, and some errors, such as describing Temporal as Cadence open-sourced and a Google-plus-AWS cloud set-up. Everything stated here is checked against public sources; the source matrix below lists each claim and its verdict.

What we would build first
