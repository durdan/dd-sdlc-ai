Geospatial index

File every location under the id of a map cell, so “what is near me” looks up a few cells and measures only what is in them.

1 · The problem

What goes wrong without it, shown happening.

“Find drivers within a kilometre” or “the nearest open shop” sounds simple. Without an index, the only honest answer is to compute the distance from the query point to every row, sort, and keep the closest. That is fine for a few hundred rows and hopeless for every driver in every city, queried many times a second.

2 · The naive fix

The obvious approach and why it breaks.

An ordinary B-tree on latitude (and another on longitude) with a bounding-box query looks like the fix. But a B-tree orders one column at a time: the latitude range returns a thin strip running right round the planet, and the longitude condition is applied afterwards to throw most of it away. Two one-dimensional indexes do not make a two-dimensional one.

3 · The pattern

Its parts and the role of each.

Turn two dimensions into one. Divide the surface into cells, give each cell an id, and store each object under the id of the cell it falls in. An ordinary index (B-tree, sorted set, hash map) then answers “who is in cell X?”. A search covers the query point's cell and its neighbours, then an exact distance check filters and sorts the candidates.

SchemeHow it cuts the globe
GeohashInterleaves latitude and longitude bits into a base-32 string along a Z-order curve; a longer shared prefix means closer (but not the reverse)
R-tree / quadtreeNested bounding boxes in a tree; PostGIS builds an R-tree on GiST, and SP-GiST supports quadtrees and k-d trees
S2Six cube faces projected onto the sphere, each cell split into four children, levels 0–30 (leaf cells about 1 cm), 64-bit ids ordered along a Hilbert curve
H3Hexagons on an icosahedron, 16 resolutions (0–15), each finer resolution about one seventh of the area; 12 pentagons at every resolution

4 · How it works

The normal flow, step by step.

The sequence uses H3 at resolution 8, whose average hexagon is about 0.74 km² with an average edge of about 0.53 km. The service finds the query point's cell, asks for gridDisk(cell, 1) (the cell plus every cell one step away), looks up the objects in those cells, computes the true distance to each, drops those beyond the radius and sorts the rest.

For moving objects such as drivers, positions arrive every few seconds, so the cell map is usually held in memory. Each update recomputes the cell; if it changed, the object moves from the old cell's set to the new one. Objects that stop reporting expire. These numbers and the in-memory design are illustrative, not taken from a specific system.

5 · When it goes wrong

Timeouts, partial failure, edge cases, and what the pattern does then.

Cell edges. Geohash proximity is one-way: nearby points can have a short or no shared prefix, notably either side of the 180° meridian, the Equator, the Greenwich meridian or near the poles. S2 documents the same one-way locality for its Hilbert-curve ids. Always search neighbouring cells, never a prefix alone. Redis does exactly this, checking the 1 + 8 areas that cover the search shape.

Hot and empty cells. Density is uneven. A city-centre cell can hold thousands of objects, giving long candidate lists and, if you shard by cell, a hot shard; use finer cells there or split the key. A rural ring can be empty; grow the ring until there are enough results or a limit is reached.

Distortion. H3 children are only approximately contained in their parents, and grid functions can return gaps when a disk crosses one of the 12 pentagons, which Uber placed in the ocean. Redis models the Earth as a sphere with the haversine formula, with up to 0.5% error in the worst case.

6 · Trade-offs

What it costs, when not to use it, and its main variants.

Choose a resolution so a cell is roughly the size of a typical search radius: too coarse and you filter long candidate lists, too fine and each query touches many cells. Geohash is the simplest and works in any database that indexes strings; R-trees handle arbitrary shapes and exact SQL predicates; S2 is strong for covering regions with cells; H3's equal-distance neighbours make rings and smoothing natural. If the data is a few thousand points in memory, a plain scan may be enough.

Which grid?

SchemeGood at · costs you
GeohashA string; prefix ranges in any index · uneven cells, seams at edges
R-tree (PostGIS GiST)Any shape, exact queries in SQL · tree updates on every move
S2Square cells, 4 children each, levels 0–30, region coverings · neighbours at two distances
H3Equal-distance neighbours, rings · children only roughly fit parents; 12 pentagons

Choosing a resolution

  • Cell about the size of a typical search radius
  • Too coarse: long candidate lists to filter
  • Too fine: many cells to look up per query
  • Dense areas may need finer cells than rural ones
  • Skip the index when the data fits in memory and a scan is fast enough

7 · In the wild

Real implementations and where our own systems use it.

Uber described H3 in June 2018, after open-sourcing it earlier that year, and uses it for ride pricing and dispatch, including measuring supply and demand per hexagon. The core is a C library under Apache 2.0 with official Java, JavaScript and Python bindings. Google's S2 Geometry library is Apache 2.0 and tested on Google's geographic data. PostGIS uses an R-tree on GiST that stores bounding boxes as a primary filter before an exact test, with SP-GiST and BRIN as alternatives. Redis GEO commands (since 3.2.0) store a 52-bit interleaved geohash as a sorted-set score. Elasticsearch has geo_point fields, geo_distance and geo_bounding_box queries, and geohash, geotile and geohex grid aggregations. MongoDB's 2dsphere index supports queries on an earth-like sphere, and $near returns documents nearest to farthest.

8 · Related patterns

What it is confused with or combined with.

A coarse cell id is a natural sharding key that keeps a neighbourhood together, at the risk of hot cities. Consistent hashing spreads cells over nodes so adding one moves few. Event streaming carries location updates into the index. A short cache in front of popular cells lets nearby queries share a lookup.

9 · Recap

Three takeaways.

Turn 2-D location into 1-D cell ids. Search your cell and its neighbours, then filter by exact distance. Size cells to your radius and density.

Recap

  • Turn 2-D location into 1-D cell ids, then use an ordinary index
  • Query your cell plus its neighbours, then filter by exact distance
  • Pick a resolution to match your radius and density
