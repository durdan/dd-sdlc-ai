Bulkhead

Split threads, connections and capacity into compartments, so one slow dependency or noisy customer can only flood its own.

1 · The problem

What goes wrong without it, shown happening.

The name comes from a ship's hull: watertight walls divide it into compartments, so a breach floods one section and the ship stays afloat. Most services have no such walls. Calls to every dependency share one thread or connection pool, so when one dependency gets slow, waiting calls hold resources until the pool is empty. Then calls to perfectly healthy services fail too. Microsoft's pattern page describes exactly this: a client's connection pool is exhausted by an unresponsive service, and eventually it cannot call any service at all.

2 · The naive fix

The obvious approach and why it breaks.

Making the shared pool bigger only postpones the outage. By Little's law, calls in flight equal arrival rate times time in the system: 100 calls a second waiting 30 seconds each needs 3,000 threads. A pool of 200 empties in about two seconds; a pool of 1,000 in about ten. Each extra thread costs memory and sends one more call to the service that is already struggling.

3 · The pattern

Its parts and the role of each.

A bulkhead caps how many calls to one dependency (or from one consumer) can be in flight at once, so a failure there can use up only its own share.

PartJob
Partition keyWhat you isolate by: dependency, tenant or customer, priority, or whole cells
LimitA thread pool or a semaphore of permits per partition
Full policyReject at once, or wait in a small bounded queue
TimeoutReturns the permit when a call hangs
FallbackWhat the caller gets when the compartment is full
MetricsIn use and rejected, per compartment

Thread-pool isolation runs the call on the bulkhead's own threads, so the caller can time out and walk away; Hystrix measured its cost at about 3 ms at the 90th percentile and 9 ms at the 99th. Semaphore isolation is only a counter, almost free, but the call runs on the caller's thread and a stuck call keeps it. Hystrix suggests semaphores for very high-volume, low-latency calls; Netflix generally preferred threads.

4 · How it works

The normal flow, step by step.

Each call takes a permit, makes the call with its own timeout, and returns the permit. When every permit is taken, the next call is rejected (Resilience4j raises BulkheadFullException) and a fallback answers. The walkthrough uses Resilience4j's semaphore Bulkhead defaults: maxConcurrentCalls 25 and maxWaitDuration 0, so a call that finds the bulkhead full is rejected immediately. Its ThreadPoolBulkhead defaults to a pool of the number of processors and a queue of 100.

5 · When it goes wrong

Timeouts, partial failure, edge cases, and what the pattern does then.

When payments slows down, its compartment fills and further charges are rejected and sent to the fallback; calls through other compartments carry on. A full bulkhead has three options: reject fast, queue in a small bounded queue to absorb a burst, or answer from a fallback. Two traps: without a timeout inside the bulkhead, a hung dependency keeps its permits for ever; and on the JVM a timed-out thread can only be interrupted, so a client that ignores interrupts can still saturate the pool (Hystrix advises setting timeouts on the HTTP client itself).

When a bulkhead is full

  • Reject fast: fail in microseconds, not in thirty seconds
  • Or queue, with a small bound, to absorb a short burst
  • Answer from a fallback the user can live with
  • Put a timeout inside it, or permits never come back

6 · Trade-offs

What it costs, when not to use it, and its main variants.

Capacity reserved for one compartment is idle while another is starved, and every compartment is a number to tune and watch. Microsoft says the pattern may not suit projects where less efficient use of resources is unacceptable, or where the added complexity is not needed. Size compartments with Hystrix's rule: peak requests per second when healthy × 99th-percentile latency in seconds, plus some breathing room, and keep them as small as that. Variants:

  • Per dependency: one pool or semaphore per downstream service.
  • Per tenant or priority: separate instances or pools so a noisy or low-priority consumer cannot starve a critical one.
  • Cell-based architecture: complete, independent copies of the workload behind a thin router keyed by customer ID. AWS: with 10 cells, a failure in one leaves 90% of requests unaffected.
  • Shuffle sharding: each customer gets a random combination of workers. With 8 workers in pairs there are 28 combinations, so a bad customer affects about 1/28 of the service, against 1/4 for four plain shards.

7 · In the wild

Real implementations and where our own systems use it.

Netflix's Hystrix isolated every dependency in its own thread pool (or semaphore); it is now in maintenance mode and Netflix recommends Resilience4j, which offers a semaphore Bulkhead and a ThreadPoolBulkhead. Polly v8 replaced its bulkhead policy with a ConcurrencyLimiter. Kubernetes applies the idea to infrastructure with per-container CPU and memory requests and limits and per-namespace ResourceQuotas. AWS builds services as cells and uses shuffle sharding: Route 53 gives each domain a shard of 4 of 2,048 virtual name servers, about 730 billion combinations, with no two domains sharing more than two. Microsoft's Azure Architecture Center has a Bulkhead pattern page and notes it is also known as cell-based architecture.

8 · Related patterns

What it is confused with or combined with.

A timeout returns the permit when a call hangs. A circuit breaker stops calling a failing dependency; the bulkhead contains the damage until it trips. Retries take permits too and should respect the limit. Fallbacks decide what the user sees. Rate limiting is the one most often confused with it: a rate limiter caps requests per unit of time, a bulkhead caps calls in flight at once.

9 · Recap

Three takeaways.

Partition what is shared. Size each compartment from throughput × latency. When it is full, reject fast and fall back.

Recap

  • Partition resources so one failure cannot drain them all
  • Size each compartment: throughput × latency, plus headroom
  • When full, reject fast and fall back; never wait for ever
