Twitter (X): the home timeline

How Twitter builds a home timeline: fan-out on write into Redis lists of tweet IDs, read-time merges for the largest accounts, Snowflake IDs for sharded storage, and the ranked For You pipeline that replaced pre-built timelines.

1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

Twitter, renamed X in 2023, lets people post short messages (tweets, now called posts) and follow other accounts. The home timeline shows recent tweets from everyone you follow (Following, reverse chronological) or a ranked mix that includes accounts you do not follow (For You).

The idea everything depends on is when to do the work. Timeline reads vastly outnumber tweets, so for years Twitter built each timeline at write time: when a tweet was posted, its ID was copied into every follower's cached timeline. The very largest accounts were the exception, merged in at read time. Since 2023 the ranked feed is assembled per request instead.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, dated. Twitter's engineering blog (16 August 2013): more than 500 million tweets a day, about 5,700 a second on average, and a one-second record of 143,199 tweets a second on 3 August 2013 in Japan, about 25 times the steady state. A write-up of Raffi Krikorian's talk Timelines at Scale (InfoQ, 3 April 2013) (secondary: High Scalability write-up of the talk) gives 300,000 timeline read requests a second against 6,000 writes, 30 billion timeline deliveries a day, and a goal of delivering a tweet within five seconds. Twitter's March 2023 post says the For You pipeline ran about 5 billion times a day. We quote no current user numbers.

Back of the envelope (ours): 30 billion deliveries ÷ 400 million tweets a day ≈ 75 timeline inserts per tweet on average, and reads outnumber writes about 50 to 1. That ratio is why the write path does the work.

The functional requirements in the video are ours, written from the product: tweets reach followers within seconds; the timeline opens fast; huge accounts and spikes do not stall others; blocked and muted content never appears; For You finds relevant tweets beyond your follows.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Inside timeline fan-out, 2012–13. All details are from the High Scalability write-up of Krikorian's talk (secondary: High Scalability write-up of the talk); the component drawing is ours.

  • Write side. Tweet ingest was synchronous and could take up to 145 ms; it was decoupled behind an asynchronous queue. Fan-out looks up the author's followers in Flock and inserts the tweet into each follower's home timeline in Redis, pipelining 4,000 destinations at a time. Each entry holds a tweet ID, a user ID and 4 bytes of flags (retweet, reply and so on). Timelines hold at most 800 entries and each is replicated on three machines. Delivery to a million followers took about 3.5 s at p50.
  • Huge accounts. The write-up lists Lady Gaga at 31 million followers; large fan-outs caused delays and out-of-order replies. For the largest accounts Twitter stopped fanning out and merged their tweets in at read time: push for most, pull for the giants.
  • Read side. The timeline service finds one machine holding the timeline, reads the IDs and hydrates them into tweets and users. Only users active within 30 days are kept in Redis; others go through reconstruction when they return.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

Post a tweet (2012–13). The API calls the tweet service, which takes an ID from Snowflake, stores the tweet and answers the author. Fan-out then runs asynchronously: followers from Flock, the tweet ID pushed onto each cached timeline and trimmed to 800. Skipping followers with no cached timeline is our reading, not stated in the source.

Serve For You (March 2023 post and code). Home Mixer, built on the Scala framework Product Mixer, takes about 1,500 candidates from a pool of hundreds of millions: in-network from the search index (Earlybird), ranked by a logistic regression model using Real Graph; out-of-network from graph traversals with GraphJet (about 15% of home timeline tweets) and from embedding spaces such as SimClusters (145,000 communities, updated every three weeks). On average the timeline was 50% in-network. A neural network of about 48 million parameters scores each candidate with ten engagement probabilities. Filters and heuristics follow (visibility filtering for blocks and mutes, author diversity, content balance, feedback fatigue, social proof, conversations, edited tweets), then ads, follow recommendations and prompts are mixed in. The pipeline completed in under 1.5 s on average and needed 220 s of CPU per run.

Snowflake (announced 1 June 2010): once tweets were hashed across databases, MySQL could not issue IDs. Requirements: tens of thousands of IDs a second (at least 10,000 per process), no coordination, roughly sortable (k-sorted within a second), 64 bits. The 2010 code builds 41 bits of milliseconds since a custom epoch (1288834974657, 4 November 2010), 5 bits of datacentre, 5 bits of worker and a 12-bit sequence, and refuses to generate IDs while the clock runs backwards. The repository was archived in 2021.

Manhattan (2 April 2014): Twitter's own multi-tenant, eventually consistent key-value database, with opt-in strong consistency (LOCAL_CAS and GLOBAL_CAS) and three storage engines: seadb (read-only, from Hadoop), sstable (write-heavy) and btree (read-heavy).

10 · Patterns used

The named patterns, each linked to its pattern walkthrough.

  • Cache-aside: timelines, tweets and users in memory, rebuilt from storage on a miss.
  • Sharding: Gizzard hashing tweets across MySQL; Redis timeline shards.
  • Event streaming: Thunder consumes new posts (its code has a Kafka module) into memory.
  • Feature flags: Decider.
  • Backpressure: built into Finagle.
  • Graceful degradation: reverse-chronological fallback for For You.

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a new social feed, not Twitter's:

  • Start with pull: query recent posts from followed accounts, indexed on (author, ID).
  • Time-ordered 64-bit IDs from day one; paginate by ID with cursors.
  • When reads dominate, add fan-out on write into capped Redis lists of IDs.
  • A follower threshold above which authors are merged at read time.
  • Hydrate IDs from a cache; never copy full posts into timelines.
  • Rank later, starting with a simple, explainable score.

What we would build first

  • Pull at first: query recent posts of followed accounts, with an index on (author, ID)
  • Time-ordered 64-bit IDs from day one; cursor pagination by ID
  • Add fan-out on write to a capped Redis list when reads dominate
  • A follower threshold above which authors are merged at read time
  • Store IDs only in timelines; hydrate from a cache
  • Ranking later: start with a simple, explainable score
