1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

YouTube lets creators upload videos and viewers watch them on phones, browsers and televisions. Advertisers pay for ads around the videos, and rights holders use Content ID to decide what happens when an upload contains their work. Google agreed to buy YouTube in October 2006, about twenty months after it was founded.

Everything in the design depends on one asymmetry: a video is prepared once and played many times. Upload and transcoding are expensive but rare; playback is constant and must be cheap and close to the viewer. Custom transcoding chips, adaptive streaming and caches inside ISPs all follow from that.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, from YouTube and Google publications. The YouTube press page says more than 20 million videos are uploaded daily on average (undated, current in October 2026). In April 2021 YouTube wrote that more than 500 hours of video are uploaded every minute on average; that figure is dated and has not been restated since in the sources we checked. In June 2025 CEO Neal Mohan said Shorts average over 200 billion daily views and that viewers watch over a billion hours a day on TVs; his January 2026 letter repeats the Shorts figure and says YouTube paid more than $100 billion to creators, artists and media companies over four years.

Back of the envelope: 20 million uploads ÷ 86,400 seconds ≈ 230 uploads a second on average.

The functional requirements in the video are ours, written from the product: uploads survive dropped connections; a new video is watchable within minutes; playback starts fast and does not stall; every device gets a format it can decode; most bytes come from a nearby cache; and copyright is checked on every upload.

3 · Context

Who and what the system talks to (C4 level 1).

Creators upload and later see analytics and revenue; viewers browse, search and play. Advertisers buy campaigns. Rights holders supply reference files to Content ID, which scans uploads and applies the owner's policy: block, monetise (sometimes sharing revenue with the uploader) or track, possibly differently per country.

The video bytes travel separately, over Google's edge network. Google describes edge nodes, Google Global Cache, as Google servers hosted inside network operators, where popular static content "including YouTube and Google Play" is temporarily cached.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Transcoding. YouTube's April 2021 post and the ASPLOS 2021 paper describe the Video (trans)Coding Unit, a custom chip whose work began in 2015. VP9 looks better than H.264 but needs five times the compute to encode, and AV1 more again; YouTube said its next-generation chip would add AV1. In live data-centre jobs the VCUs were 20–33× more efficient than YouTube's tuned software baseline, and YouTube said videos became available promptly after upload even during surges (daily livestreams grew 45% in the first half of 2020).

The pipeline around the chip is our design: split at keyframes; one task per chunk and rendition on a priority queue with low resolutions first; chips plus software encoders for overflow; stitch and check; package into DASH segments; mark each format ready. YouTube's help page confirms the visible behaviour: a new upload is processed in low quality first, and a one-hour 4K 30 fps video can take up to four hours to finish high-resolution processing.

Recommendations. The 2016 RecSys paper, "Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations", describes a funnel of two networks. Candidate generation uses watch and search history, demographics and context to pick hundreds of videos from millions, served as a nearest-neighbour search over embeddings. Ranking scores those hundreds, plus candidates from other sources, and is trained to predict expected watch time rather than click probability. The models had about a billion parameters and were built on TensorFlow. This is a 2016 snapshot.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

Upload to watchable. Following the Data API's resumable upload protocol, the client starts a session and receives a session URI. If the connection drops, it sends an empty PUT with Content-Range: bytes */TOTAL; the server answers 308 Resume Incomplete with a Range header, and the client sends only the remaining bytes. Clients should retry 500, 502, 503 and 504 responses with exponential backoff. After that, our design fans out: store the original, queue transcoding and Content ID in parallel, publish low quality first, HD later.

Press play. The player fetches the formats for a video, then requests short segments from a nearby cache. Google's HTML5 case study says YouTube delivers H.264 and VP9 through MPEG-DASH and uses Media Source Extensions to switch bitrates as bandwidth changes; with adaptive bitrate and VP9, buffering fell by more than 50% globally and up to 80% on heavily congested networks. 7 · Data and caching

What is stored where, for how long, and what is never stored.

Google describes three tiers: data centres for computation and storage; edge points of presence at over 100 interconnection facilities, where Google peers with other networks; and edge nodes (Google Global Cache) hosted by operators. Google provides the GGC hardware; the ISP provides rack space, power and connectivity. Google says typically 70–90% of cacheable traffic can be served from GGC, and that users are served from the best available location during outages.

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a small video platform, not YouTube's:

  • Resumable uploads (for example tus or a cloud provider's resumable API) straight to object storage.
  • A queue of transcode jobs producing three H.264 sizes, smallest first, on ordinary machines.
  • HLS or DASH segments behind a commercial CDN; no edge hardware of our own.
  • Postgres for videos and comments, keyed by video id so it can be sharded later.
  • Recommendations from subscriptions and what is popular this week; learn from watch time later.
  • Per-user upload limits and a copyright report flow from the first day.

What we would build first

  • Resumable uploads straight to object storage
  • A queue of transcode jobs: three H.264 sizes, low first
  • HLS or DASH segments behind a commercial CDN
  • Postgres for videos and comments, keyed by video id
  • Recommendations: subscriptions and popular-this-week
  • Per-user upload limits from day one
