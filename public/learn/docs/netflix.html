1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

Netflix streaming serves more than 325 million paid memberships (passed in Q4 2025). The design rests on one split. The control plane runs in AWS: sign-in, browsing, recommendations, playback decisions, and choosing which server should send each stream. The data plane is Open Connect, Netflix's own content delivery network of purpose-built appliances (OCAs) placed at internet exchanges and inside internet providers' networks. OCAs only serve files and report their status; everything else is decided in the cloud.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

The scale numbers are Netflix's own, from the Q4 2025 and Q2 2026 shareholder letters. The latest letters do not give a quarterly membership count, so 325M+ (Q4 2025) is the latest figure. The average concurrency is our arithmetic: 97 billion hours ÷ (181 days × 24 hours) ≈ 22 million streams playing at any moment, on average; peaks are much higher. The requirements list is ours, written from the product rather than from a Netflix document.

What the system must do (our requirements)

  • Video starts within a couple of seconds of pressing play
  • No stalls: quality drops before playback stops
  • Works on thousands of device types, old and new
  • Bytes travel the shortest network distance possible
  • A failed server, zone or cloud region never stops playback
  • Browsing degrades gracefully when a service is down

3 · Context

Who and what the system talks to (C4 level 1).

Members use Netflix apps on TVs, phones, laptops and set-top boxes. Studios and partners deliver masters. AWS hosts the control plane; Netflix finished its cloud migration in January 2016, seven years after a 2008 database corruption stopped DVD shipping for three days. Internet providers and exchanges host the Open Connect appliances: embedded OCAs are provided free of charge to qualifying ISPs, which supply power, space and connectivity, and Netflix also peers at exchanges (settlement-free interconnection).

4 · Containers

The deployable parts and 3–5 paths through them (C4 level 2).

Four paths matter. Browse: the app calls the Zuul gateway, Netflix's front door for all requests into its cloud, which routes to browse services reading from EVCache (memcached-based) and Cassandra. Play: Zuul routes to playback services, which check rights and choose streams; steering picks OCAs using cache control's view of every appliance. Stream: the app fetches segments straight from an OCA over HTTPS. Fill: encoded files go to S3, then onto OCAs in off-peak windows. Service discovery inside the control plane historically used Eureka, Netflix's AWS service registry. The grouping into "browse" and "playback" services is our simplification of many microservices.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Netflix's Open Connect overview lists the playback steps: OCAs periodically report health, learned BGP routes and file availability to cache control in AWS; playback services check authorisation and licensing and pick files for the client and network; the steering service uses cache control's data to pick OCAs and generate URLs; playback services hand the URLs to the client. A 2024 Netflix presentation says received routes are synchronised with the control plane about every five minutes. A 2018 Netflix presentation describes steering on BGP attributes (longest match, AS path, MED) with embedded OCAs preferred over peering, and MED offsets that prefer private over public peering; treat those specifics as dated. Fill uses manifest clusters, fill clusters, elected fill masters and tiered peer-to-peer copying. The "popularity and fill planner" box is our grouping.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

Press play. The device asks playback services (via Zuul) to play a title; rights are checked; streams are chosen for the device; steering returns ranked OCA URLs; the app streams from an OCA and adapts its bitrate to measured throughput; telemetry returns to the control plane. Returning several URLs so the player can fail over on its own is our reading of "generates URLs for these OCAs", and the licence step is our sketch.

A title reaches the CDN. Content operations run QC and encoding and deploy every asset to S3. OCAs ask the control plane for a manifest; elected fill masters download from S3 in their off-peak window and report back; other OCAs are pointed to the masters and copy tier by tier as the fill window moves across time zones. When enough clusters hold enough copies, the title is live for serving.

7 · Data and caching

What is stored where, for how long, and what is never stored.

Member and account data lives in Cassandra, replicated asynchronously between regions, with EVCache in front; some calculated data exists only in the cache (Netflix, 2013). Encoded files live in S3; copies sit on OCA disks, placed by popularity and spread across a cluster with consistent hashing (each server ID hashed 1,000 times onto the ring), later weighted for mixed storage and flash servers. OCAs never store member data, viewing history or DRM information.

Encodes are the bulk of what is stored per title. The 2010 fixed ladder topped out at 5,800 kbps for 1080p; per-title encoding (2015) gave each title its own ladder; the 2018 dynamic optimizer described shot-based encoding judged by VMAF; in December 2025 Netflix reported AV1 at about 30% of viewing, its second most used codec.

8 · Growth

How the same design grows from launch to its current scale.

The shape has been stable for a decade; what changed is scale and the parts underneath. Netflix moved to AWS from 2008 to 2016, started Open Connect in 2011, went active-active across two US regions in 2013, open-sourced Zuul 2 and Titus in 2018 (the Titus repository was archived in 2022), replaced Asgard with Spinnaker for continuous delivery (2015), and in 2025 described carrying live events over the same Open Connect network, which it put at more than 6,000 locations, linked to five AWS regions by its own backbone.

9 · Failure modes

What breaks, what users see, and what protects them.

Published protections: OCAs fail over to other embedded sites or exchange appliances; regions fail over by moving traffic to healthy regions, rehearsed with Chaos Kong; adaptive bitrate lowers quality before stalling; Hystrix popularised fallbacks and graceful degradation. For live events Netflix reports up to ten times the load from members restarting streams after interruptions of as little as 30 seconds. The browse fallback (a non-personalised row) and retry budgets for on-demand are our design.

Netflix tests these by breaking things on purpose: Chaos Monkey (2011) terminates production instances at random; Latency Monkey injects delays; Chaos Gorilla simulates the loss of an availability zone; Chaos Kong (2015) evacuates a whole region, which Netflix credits with sidestepping the September 2015 DynamoDB outage in US-East-1.

What fails, and what members see

FailureWhat protects members
An appliance or ISP site goes downSteered to other sites or exchange appliances; app has spare URLs
Network gets congestedAdaptive bitrate: lower quality, no stall
A browse service failsFallback, e.g. a non-personalised row (our design)
A whole AWS region failsTraffic evacuated to healthy regions; rehearsed with Chaos Kong
Members mass-retry after a glitchThrottling at Zuul and retry budgets (our design for VOD)

Breaking it on purpose

  • Chaos Monkey (2011): randomly terminates production instances
  • Latency Monkey: injects delays between services
  • Chaos Gorilla: simulates losing a whole availability zone
  • Chaos Kong (2015): evacuates a whole AWS region
  • Live events: failure injection, from bad segments to a lost region

10 · Patterns used

The named patterns, each linked to its pattern walkthrough.

Circuit breaker with fallback (Hystrix, now in maintenance mode; Netflix points new projects to Resilience4j and adaptive concurrency limits) — see the circuit breaker walkthrough. Bulkhead, via Hystrix's thread and semaphore isolation — bulkhead. Timeouts and retries, including Zuul 2 retrying failed requests on other servers — timeout, retry. Rate limiting at the gateway (Zuul 2 throttling and brownout filters). Consistent hashing for content placement on OCAs. Plus active-active regions and proactive caching.

Patterns used

  • Circuit breaker and fallback: Hystrix, now in maintenance mode
  • Bulkhead: Hystrix's thread and semaphore isolation
  • Timeout and retry: around every remote call; retry on another server
  • Rate limiting: throttling at the Zuul gateway
  • Consistent hashing: spreading files across appliances in a cluster
  • Active-active regions and proactive caching

11 · Key decisions

The choices that shaped it, with the alternatives and trade-offs.

Own CDN versus commercial CDNs: Netflix built Open Connect so it could work directly with ISPs and use proactive, directed caching that it says cuts upstream demand by several orders of magnitude, at the cost of designing and running hardware worldwide. Cloud versus own data centres: the control plane runs in AWS. Off-peak fill versus fill on demand: only possible because viewing is predictable. Active-active versus standby: requires stateless services and asynchronous replication, with no cross-region calls on the user's path. Per-title and shot-based encoding: more compute, less bandwidth for every stream.

Key decisions

DecisionWhy, and the alternative
Own CDN, not only commercial CDNsProactive caching inside ISPs; costs hardware and operations
Control plane in AWS, not own data centresElastic capacity after the 2008 outage; depends on one provider
Fill off-peak, not on demandPopularity is predictable; needs good forecasts
Active-active regions, not standbyFailover is rehearsed; data replication is asynchronous
Encode per title and per shotLess bandwidth for the same quality; much more compute

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendation, not Netflix's: keep the two-plane split from day one but rent the data plane. A commercial CDN with signed URLs; one API service for catalogue, rights and playback behind a managed gateway; Postgres plus a cache; per-title encoding packaged for HLS and DASH; more than one CDN URL per play so the player can fail over; timeouts, retries with backoff and breakers on every remote call. Build your own appliances only when traffic makes the economics obvious.

What we would build first

  • A commercial CDN with signed URLs; our own appliances only at real scale
  • One API service for catalogue, rights and playback, behind a managed gateway
  • Postgres for accounts and rights; a cache in front of catalogue reads
  • Encode with a per-title ladder, packaged for HLS and DASH
  • Return two or three CDN URLs per play; let the player fail over
  • Timeouts, retries with backoff, and breakers on every remote call
