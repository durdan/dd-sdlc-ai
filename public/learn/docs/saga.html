Saga

Keep data consistent across services with a sequence of local transactions, undoing completed steps with compensating actions when a later one fails.

1 · The problem

What goes wrong without it, shown happening.

With a database per service, one business operation such as placing an order touches several services, each committing to its own store. There is no single transaction around them. If a later step fails (stock runs out after the card was charged), each database is consistent on its own but together they are wrong: the customer has paid for an order that will never ship. The figures in the walkthrough (a £40 order, four services) are illustrative.

2 · The naive fix

The obvious approach and why it breaks.

The textbook answer is a distributed transaction using two-phase commit: a coordinator asks every participant to prepare (promise to commit), and only then tells them all to commit. It is a blocking protocol: participants hold their resources while they wait, and if the coordinator fails after they vote yes, they cannot resolve the transaction alone. AWS's guidance notes that in a database-per-service design 2PC "is not an option", and many data stores, including most NoSQL ones, do not provide it. microservices.io lists "2PC is not an option" among the forces that lead to sagas.

3 · The pattern

Its parts and the role of each.

A saga is a sequence of local transactions. Each one commits in a single service and triggers the next step through an event or message. If a step fails, the saga runs compensating transactions for the steps already completed, in reverse order.

PartJob
Compensable stepA local transaction that can be undone by another with the opposite effect (reserve stock → release stock)
Compensating transactionA semantic undo: a new business action such as a refund, not a database rollback
PivotThe point of no return; once it succeeds, the saga only goes forward
Retryable stepAn idempotent step after the pivot, retried until it succeeds
CoordinationChoreography (services react to each other's events) or orchestration (a coordinator issues commands)

Garcia-Molina and Salem's 1987 paper is explicit that a compensating transaction "undoes, from a semantic point of view" the original step but "does not necessarily return the database to the state that existed" before it, because other transactions may have run in between.

Compensation is not rollback

RollbackCompensation
The change never happenedThe change happened and was seen
Database restores old valuesBusiness action with the opposite effect
Charge disappearsRefund appears beside the charge
AutomaticYou design, test and run it

4 · How it works

The normal flow, step by step.

In the orchestrated version, an orchestrator creates the order as pending, then sends commands in turn (reserve stock, charge card, hand to courier) and records each outcome before moving on, so it can resume after a crash. Once the pivot (the courier collection, in this illustrative example) succeeds, the remaining steps are retried until done and the order is confirmed. In the choreographed version, the same flow is a chain of events: OrderCreated, StockReserved, PaymentCaptured, ShipmentBooked. Each step must update its database and publish its event atomically; microservices.io points to the transactional outbox or event sourcing for this.

5 · When it goes wrong

Timeouts, partial failure, edge cases, and what the pattern does then.

When a step before the pivot fails, the saga compensates the completed steps newest first: refund, release stock, cancel the order. Compensations must keep retrying until they succeed (Temporal's documentation advises generous retry policies and no workflow-level timeout) and must be idempotent, because messages and retries can deliver them twice, and they may run when the forward step never happened. Microsoft warns that compensations can fail and leave the system inconsistent, so they need monitoring.

The deeper problem is isolation: each local transaction commits immediately, so others can see a half-finished saga. Microsoft lists lost updates, dirty reads and fuzzy (non-repeatable) reads, and countermeasures: semantic locks (mark the record pending), commutative updates, a pessimistic view (move updates into retryable steps after the pivot), rereading values before updating, version files, and choosing by risk. The 1987 paper already notes that no effort is made to notify or abort transactions that saw a step's results before it was compensated.

No isolation: and what to do about it

  • Others can see a half-finished saga
  • Semantic lock: mark records PENDING
  • Commutative updates: order does not matter
  • Reread before writing; restart if it changed
  • Put risky updates after the pivot

6 · Trade-offs

What it costs, when not to use it, and its main variants.

Choreography needs no extra service and has no single coordinator to fail, but the flow is spread across handlers, risks cyclic dependencies, and is hard to integration-test. Orchestration keeps the flow in one place and avoids cycles, at the cost of building the coordinator and depending on it. Both give eventual, not immediate, consistency and need every step to have a designed compensation. Microsoft advises against sagas when transactions are tightly coupled or have cyclic dependencies; if the data lives in one database, a local transaction is simpler.

Choreography or orchestration

ChoreographyOrchestration
No extra service to runAn orchestrator to build and keep up
Good for a few simple stepsGood for long or changing flows
Flow is spread across servicesFlow is in one place
Risk of cyclic dependenciesOrchestrator is a point of failure
Hard to test end to endEasier to trace and monitor

The cost, and when not to use it

  • Eventual consistency: users see PENDING
  • Every step needs a designed undo
  • Hard to debug as participants grow
  • Skip it inside one database: use a transaction
  • Skip it if steps are tightly coupled or cyclic

7 · In the wild

Real implementations and where our own systems use it.

Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem (Princeton) introduced sagas in "Sagas", SIGMOD 1987, for long-lived transactions in one database. microservices.io describes choreography- and orchestration-based sagas across services, with an order and customer-credit example. Microsoft's Azure Architecture Center defines compensable, pivot and retryable transactions. AWS Prescriptive Guidance publishes a Step Functions sample with Place Order, Update Inventory and Make Payment steps and Revert Payment, Revert Inventory and Remove Order compensations. Temporal, which originated as a fork of Uber's Cadence, documents a saga pattern where compensations are registered as steps run and executed in reverse order on failure.

8 · Related patterns

What it is confused with or combined with.

Database per service is what creates the need for sagas. The transactional outbox and event sourcing make "commit and publish" atomic. Idempotent consumers make duplicate deliveries harmless. CQRS read models are often fed by the same events. Two-phase commit is the atomic alternative that sagas avoid.

Related patterns

Transactional outboxCommit a step and its event together
Idempotent consumerA step that runs twice does no harm

CQRSRead models built from the saga's events
Two-phase commitThe atomic alternative sagas avoid

9 · Recap

Three takeaways.

A saga is a sequence of local transactions. Undo with compensations, which are new business actions, not rollbacks. There is no isolation, so design for it with pending states and a pivot, and make every step idempotent.

Recap

  • One local transaction per service, in sequence
  • Undo with compensations: new actions, not rollback
  • No isolation: design for it, and make every step idempotent
