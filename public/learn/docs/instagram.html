Instagram: photos at planet scale

How a photo app grew from one server to billions of users on a familiar stack: Django, PostgreSQL split into thousands of logical shards, 64-bit IDs that encode time and shard, Redis feed lists, memcached and a CDN.

1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

Instagram launched on 6 October 2010 as a photo-sharing app, joined Facebook in April 2012 and now runs on Meta's infrastructure. People post photos and videos, scroll a feed of accounts they follow, and browse Explore and Reels for accounts they do not.

The idea that carries the design: keep canonical data in a database the team understands (PostgreSQL), split it early into many small logical shards, and give every object a 64-bit ID that encodes when it was made and which shard holds it. Everything heavy (image bytes, feed lists, hot objects) is kept off that database.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, dated:

  • 25,000 sign-ups on launch day; 30+ million users in under two years (Mike Krieger, SFPUG talk, April 2012).
  • 1 million accounts by December 2010; 10 million by September 2011; 1 billion monthly users by June 2018 (Instagram's own timeline).
  • 14 million users run by three engineers on 100+ EC2 instances, December 2011 (Instagram's "What Powers Instagram" post, via a secondary summary).
  • 3 billion monthly active users, announced by Mark Zuckerberg on 24 September 2025 (reported by the press; Meta stopped per-app reporting in 2024).
  • "Meta's family of apps serves trillions of image download requests every day" (Meta, March 2024; all apps, not Instagram alone).

The functional requirements in the video are ours, written from the product: posting feels instant; the feed opens fast and is relevant; accepted posts are never lost; images load quickly everywhere; IDs sort by time without a lookup (one of Instagram's own 2012 ID requirements); capacity grows without downtime.

3 · Context

Who and what the system talks to (C4 level 1).

People use the app; API requests go to Instagram, while image and video bytes come from a CDN (Amazon CloudFront in 2011, Meta's own today). Notifications go out through Apple's and Google's push services. Advertisers place ads across Feed, Stories and Reels. Since the 2013–14 migration, Instagram runs in Facebook's (now Meta's) data centres and uses its shared systems.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Sharding and IDs, from Mike Krieger's "Sharding @ Instagram" talk (SFPUG, April 2012) and the matching "Sharding & IDs at Instagram" post.

  • Before sharding: one PostgreSQL primary with streaming replicas, then vertical partitioning (photos into their own database) using Django database routers. The photos database passed 60 GB on EC2 machines with at most 68 GB of RAM.
  • Logical shards: thousands of logical shards, each a PostgreSQL schema, mapped to far fewer machines. shard_key % num_logical_shards picks the shard (usually the user ID); a map picks the machine. Instagram replaced the Django ORM for sharded data with a small layer: select(fields, table_name, shard_key, where_statement, where_parameters). PgBouncer pools connections and hides moves from the application.
  • Alternatives rejected: NoSQL stores, Skype's sharding proxy and range or date partitioning; for IDs, ticket servers, UUIDs, Twitter's Snowflake and application-made IDs.
  • ID layout: 41 bits of milliseconds since a custom epoch (1314220021721 ms, 24 August 2011, about 69 years of range), 13 bits of logical shard ID (8,192 shards), and 10 bits of sequence (nextval(...) % 1024, so 1,024 IDs per shard per millisecond). A PL/pgSQL next_id() in each schema builds it; the shard is recovered with bit shifts. Pros given: unique in 64 bits with little CPU cost; con: large IDs from the start.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

Both sequences are our reconstruction from the parts Instagram described in 2011–12.

Posting a photo. The app uploads the photo and caption; the bytes go to media storage; the media row is inserted into the author's shard, whose next_id() returns the ID. Django enqueues a fan-out task and replies at once. A worker reads the author's followers (SMEMBERS followers:<id>) and pushes the media ID onto each follower's list (LPUSH feed:<user>), as shown in Krieger's 2012 slides, then sends notifications.

Opening the feed. Django reads a page of media IDs from Redis (LRANGE). Since 2016 a ranking model orders candidates (Instagram says people were missing 70% of posts in a newest-first feed). Feed uses thousands of signals and about a dozen predictions such as liking, commenting or spending a few seconds on a post. Posts are multi-fetched from memcached; misses go to the shard read from each ID's bits, then refill the cache. The app fetches images from the CDN.

Resharding, per the 2012 talk: clone a machine with repmgr standby clone, promote the copy, and point half of the logical shards at it. User IDs still map to the same logical shard, so nothing is re-hashed. Rolling out a new map needed about 30 seconds of maintenance, and the scheme works only while logical shards outnumber machines. Before resharding, pg_reorg saved 20+ GB on some databases.

10 · Patterns used

The named patterns, each linked to its pattern walkthrough.

  • Sharding: many more logical shards than machines.
  • Cache-aside: memcached in front of PostgreSQL.
  • Read replicas: PostgreSQL streaming replication, for failover and to reduce contention.
  • CDN caching: image bytes from the edge.
  • Competing consumers: workers pulling fan-out and notification jobs from a queue.

Patterns used

  • Sharding: thousands of logical PostgreSQL shards on fewer machines
  • Cache-aside: memcached in front of PostgreSQL
  • Read replicas: streaming replication, replicas in another zone
  • CDN caching: images served from the edge, originals in storage

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a new photo-sharing product, not Instagram's:

  • One web app and PostgreSQL; photos in object storage behind a CDN, never through the app servers on read.
  • 64-bit, time-sortable IDs with a shard field from day one.
  • Hundreds of logical shards (schemas) on one machine, so splitting later is a map change.
  • A queue and workers for fan-out, image sizes and notifications.
  • Redis for feed lists; memcached or Redis for hot objects, cache-aside.
  • A newest-first feed until people start missing posts; then ranking.

What we would build first

  • One web app, PostgreSQL, object storage behind a CDN
  • 64-bit time-sortable IDs with a shard field, from day one
  • Hundreds of logical shards on one machine, ready to split
  • A queue and workers for fan-out, image sizes and notifications
  • Redis for feed lists, memcached or Redis for hot objects
  • Newest-first feed; add ranking once people miss posts
