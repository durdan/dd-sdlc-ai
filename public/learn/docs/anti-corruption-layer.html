Anti-corruption layer

Translate a vendor's or legacy system's model into your own at one boundary, so their changes and quirks stop rippling through your code.

1 · The problem

What goes wrong without it, shown happening.

Integrating with a system you do not control (a shipping carrier, a payment provider, a legacy ERP) tempts every team to use its model directly: its field names, its status codes, its units, its identifiers as your primary keys. Each use is small. Together they mean the other system's model has become yours. When it changes, every service that touched it breaks at once, on their timetable. Switching vendors, or adding a second one, stops being a change and becomes a rewrite. Evans' description: a large interface with an upstream system can eventually overwhelm the downstream model, causing it to be modified to resemble the other system's model in an ad hoc fashion.

2 · The naive fix

The obvious approach and why it breaks.

A shared client library removes duplicated HTTP code but usually returns the vendor's own types. It hides the transport, not the model, so a renamed field still changes every caller, and a second vendor with a different model does not fit behind it.

3 · The pattern

Its parts and the role of each.

An anti-corruption layer is an isolating layer that gives your system the other system's functionality in terms of your own model. It talks to the other system through its existing interface, needing little or no change there, and translates in one or both directions. Microsoft describes it as a facade or adapter layer between subsystems that do not share the same semantics; it can be a component inside the application or an independent service.

PartJob
FacadeThe interface your code calls, in your terms only
TranslatorMaps fields, status codes, units and errors between the two models
ID mapYour own identifiers, mapped to theirs; never their ids as your keys
AdapterTransport: HTTP, authentication, timeouts, retries, circuit breaker
Event receiverTheir webhooks or messages in; verified, de-duplicated, translated into your events

The carrier, its codes (D1–D9, D3, X9, EXP) and the numbers in this walkthrough are illustrative.

4 · How it works

The normal flow, step by step.

Outbound, your service asks the facade for something in your terms; the translator builds the other system's request (units, codes, field names); the adapter sends it; the reply's identifiers are recorded against yours in the ID map; your caller gets an answer in your model. Inbound, the same layer receives their events, checks and de-duplicates them, looks up your identifiers, and publishes your own events. Microsoft notes the layer can work synchronously or asynchronously through messaging.

5 · When it goes wrong

Timeouts, partial failure, edge cases, and what the pattern does then.

Because every call to the other system passes through the layer, it is the natural place for their failures. AWS warns that a failing layer makes the target unreachable and recommends retries with backoff and circuit breakers, plus alerts and logging. Return failures to callers in your own terms. Do not pass unknown codes through or guess: park the message and alert, so someone adds the mapping. Microsoft suggests input validation and sanitisation at this boundary, and observability such as correlation ids and structured logging to diagnose translation failures.

Test the translator with recorded responses, including the awkward ones. Contract tests, as Martin Fowler describes them, run separately and periodically to check that your test doubles still return what the real service would.

Testing the layer

  • Unit-test the translator with recorded carrier responses
  • Include the odd ones: unknown codes, nulls, error bodies
  • Run contract tests against the real sandbox on a schedule
  • A failing contract test means their model moved, not ours

6 · Trade-offs

What it costs, when not to use it, and its main variants.

Microsoft and AWS list the costs: added latency, another service to manage and maintain (monitoring, releases, CI/CD), a possible scaling bottleneck and single point of failure, and the need to keep transactions and data consistent. If the layer exists for a migration, decide whether it is permanent or will be retired; AWS suggests recording an interim layer as technical debt. Microsoft says the pattern may not be suitable when the two systems have no significant semantic differences, and advises keeping business rules and orchestration out of the layer. Variants: a component inside the caller or a separate service; one shared layer or one per integration.

7 · In the wild

Real implementations and where our own systems use it.

Eric Evans named the pattern in Domain-Driven Design (2003) among the patterns for relating bounded contexts; his free DDD Reference keeps the definition. Martin Fowler's Bounded Context article explains why large systems have several models that must be mapped at their boundaries. Microsoft's Azure Architecture Center credits Evans and shows an implementation with API Management and Azure Functions. AWS Prescriptive Guidance uses the layer while moving a monolith to microservices, with a sample repository.

8 · Related patterns

What it is confused with or combined with.

Adapter and facade are the building blocks inside the layer. An API gateway also sits at an edge, but it routes and protects traffic rather than owning a translation between models. The strangler fig pattern migrates a legacy system piece by piece, and the layer is how new and old talk meanwhile. Contract tests check the layer's assumptions about the other side; circuit breakers and retries handle that side's failures.

9 · Recap

Three takeaways.

Don't let their model become yours. Translate at one boundary, in both directions. Handle their failures and quirks there too.

Recap

  • Don't let their model become yours
  • Translate at one boundary: ids, statuses, units, events
  • Handle their failures and quirks there too
