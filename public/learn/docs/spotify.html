Spotify: press play anywhere

How a music service starts a song in a moment, hands it to a speaker, and counts every play: audio files on CDNs, a phone that becomes a remote, and an event pipeline on Google Cloud Pub/Sub that carries more than a trillion events a day.

1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

Spotify streams music, podcasts and audiobooks to apps on phones, desktops and the web, and to speakers, TVs, cars and consoles through Spotify Connect. A listener presses play and the song starts almost at once; they can then move it to another device and carry on from the same second.

The idea everything depends on: playing audio is the cheap, static part; counting it is the hard part. Audio is a set of unchanging files served from delivery networks near the listener. Every play is also an event that must be counted exactly once, because events such as EndSong are used to pay royalties to labels and artists.

Organisation, briefly: in 2014 Spotify described its engineering culture of small autonomous teams called squads, and was clear it was "a journey in progress". This case study is about the systems, not the org chart.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, from Spotify's own publications. Its Q2 2026 results (published 4 August 2026) reported 777 million monthly active users, up 12% year on year, and 300 million Premium subscribers, up 9%. Its company page lists 184 markets and over 100 million tracks, 7 million podcast titles and 700,000 audiobooks. In May 2024 its data platform team wrote that it collects more than one trillion events a day across more than 1,800 event types.

Back of the envelope: 1 trillion ÷ 86,400 seconds ≈ 11.6 million events a second on average; peaks are higher. In 2019 the peak was already over 8 million a second.

The functional requirements in the video are ours: start a song in well under a second on a weak network; keep playing through short gaps; move playback between devices without losing the place; count every finished play exactly once; keep personal data out of analytics; and let hundreds of teams add events without a central bottleneck.

3 · Context

Who and what the system talks to (C4 level 1).

The listener's app talks to Spotify to browse, search and choose what to play, but the audio comes from elsewhere: each encoded track is stored as files on HTTP servers around the world, using several CDN partners (2018). Spotify sends commands to Connect devices, and third-party apps can control a Premium listener's playback through the Web API (originally launched in 2017 as the Spotify Connect Web API).

Artists, labels and podcasters deliver content; Spotify prefers FLAC and handles all format conversions. Underneath is Google Cloud: Spotify began exploring a cloud strategy in early 2015, Google announced the partnership on 24 February 2016, traffic was fully routed to Google Cloud by May 2017, the first of four data centres closed in December 2017 and the remaining three in 2018.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Inside event delivery, from Spotify's posts of 2016, 2019, 2021 and 2024. The parts are Spotify's; the topic names and the low-priority example are ours.

  • Control plane. Teams declare event types and Avro schemas and bind them to SDKs. A central provisioner creates a Pub/Sub topic and subscription, processing and storage for each type; schema changes update the matching resources.
  • Receive. SDKs send events with message identifiers to the receiver service, which recognises the type and publishes it; malformed or unknown events are rejected. During an outage clients store events and resend them under a retry policy (2021).
  • Isolate. Each type has its own topic, processing and storage, so noisy events cannot disrupt business-critical ones. Types are ranked high, normal or low, with targets of a few hours, 24 hours and 72 hours (2019).
  • Clean and store. Deduplication on message identifiers can look back across weeks; personal fields are pseudonymised or encrypted according to schema annotations; data lands in immutable hourly buckets. Spotify chose "liveness over lateness": one type's open hour does not block others.

Scale over time: about 700,000 events a second at peak in 2016; 500 billion a day, 8 million a second at peak, 500+ types, 350 TB a day on about 2,500 VMs in 2019; over a trillion a day and 1,800+ types in 2024, feeding more than 38,000 scheduled pipelines.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

Press play. The app asks the backend for the track; what comes back (metadata and which file to fetch) is our design. The app fetches the file from a nearby server in chunks with HTTP GET range requests; Spotify's 2018 post gives a typical chunk of 512 kB. It keeps fetching as the buffer drains, and sends events: playback started, and EndSong when the track finishes. In a 2018 A/B test on one CDN, the BBR congestion control algorithm cut stutter by 6–10% with no change in latency.

Move to a speaker. The speaker is online on the listener's account (and discoverable locally with ZeroConf). The phone lists devices and calls transfer playback; the public Web API's PUT /v1/me/player takes a single device id and works only for Premium users. The speaker must receive a became-active notification before it may play; only the active device plays audio, and the phone continues as a remote for pause, skip and volume. Routing commands through a server-side state service is our design; that the speaker fetches its own audio is our reading.

10 · Patterns used

The named patterns, each linked to its pattern walkthrough.

  • Publish–subscribe: one Pub/Sub topic per event type.
  • Event streaming: over a trillion events a day into hourly datasets.
  • CDN caching: unchanging audio files served near listeners.
  • Bulkhead: per-type isolation with priority tiers.
  • Idempotency: deduplication by event message identifier.
  • Retry: clients keep and resend events during outages.

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a small music service, not Spotify's:

  • Encoded files per track and quality in object storage behind one CDN; players fetch with range requests and buffer ahead.
  • One API service and Postgres for catalogue, accounts and playback state.
  • An event endpoint where clients attach a message identifier; deduplicate on a unique key.
  • A separate managed topic for each business-critical event type from day one.
  • Hourly batches for play counts and royalties; real-time analytics later.

What we would build first

  • Store each track as files per quality on object storage, behind one CDN
  • Players fetch with HTTP range requests and buffer ahead
  • One API service and Postgres for catalogue, accounts and playback state
  • An event endpoint with client message ids; dedupe on a unique key
  • A managed topic per critical event type from day one
  • Hourly batches for play counts; no real-time analytics yet
