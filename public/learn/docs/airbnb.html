Airbnb: search to booking

How a two-sided marketplace takes a guest from a search to a paid booking: two-stage search, a server-driven UI, change data capture, and payments wrapped so that a retry never charges twice.

1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

Airbnb is a marketplace. Hosts list rooms, flats and houses (and, more recently, experiences and services); guests search, book and pay. Airbnb takes the guest's payment through payment providers and pays the host out.

Everything depends on one promise made to two people at once: a booking happens exactly once. The guest must not be charged twice when a call to a bank times out, and the host's nights must not be sold twice. Airbnb's Orpheus idempotency library and its rebuilt payment orchestration exist to keep the first half; the second half is shown in this video as our design.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, from Airbnb's own shareholder letters. The Q4 2025 letter (12 February 2026) reports 533.0 million Nights and Seats Booked in 2025, up 8%, $91.3 billion of gross booking value and $12.2 billion of revenue, and more than 9 million active listings at the end of 2025. The Q2 2026 letter (August 2026) reports 148.3 million Nights and Seats Booked in the quarter, up 10%, over 5.5 million hosts, over 2.5 billion guest arrivals all time, and 64% of nights booked in the app. Airbnb's newsroom (May 2026) lists listings in 220+ countries and regions.

Back of the envelope: 533 million ÷ 31.5 million seconds ≈ 17 nights booked a second, on average. Airbnb does not publish nights per booking, so the number of bookings a second is not derived here.

The functional requirements in the video are ours, written from the product: fast search by place, dates and guests; no night sold twice; no guest charged twice; new screens on every platform without app releases; changes spread within seconds; and local ways to pay.

3 · Context

Who and what the system talks to (C4 level 1).

Two kinds of user: guests and hosts. Airbnb answers each request and pushes booking requests, messages and confirmations to both sides.

The money side is simplified. Airbnb charges guests through payment service providers that reach card networks and issuing banks, and pays hosts out to a bank account or other payout method. Airbnb's Pay as a Local post counts more than 300 payment options worldwide and describes launching 20+ local methods.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

One payment step, wrapped in Orpheus, from “Avoiding Double Payments in a Distributed Payments System” by Jon Chew and Ninad Khisti (16 April 2019). Showing Reservations as the caller is our choice.

  • Three phases. Pre-RPC records the request; RPC makes the network call; Post-RPC records the outcome. No network calls in pre- or post-RPC; no database work in the RPC phase.
  • Errors. 5xx infrastructure errors are retryable; 4xx validation errors are not, and every retry should fail the same way. A custom exception defaults to non-retryable. Mislabelling causes permanent failures one way and double payments the other.
  • Keys and leases. Clients persist their key before calling. Request-level keys are random (a UUID); entity-level keys are deterministic, such as payment-1234-refund. Each request takes a row-level lock that acts as a lease with an expiry longer than the RPC timeout; without a response, a retry may run only after the lease expires.
  • Primary only. Idempotency data is read and written on the master (primary) database, never replicas, because replica lag could make a retry look new. The tables are sharded by idempotency key.
  • One commit. Orpheus's writes and the service's own writes commit in one transaction, combined with Java lambdas rather than nested transactions.

Airbnb reports five nines of consistency for payments since Orpheus launched, while annual payment volume doubled.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

A search, then a listing page (calls are our design). The app sends place, dates and guests through Viaduct to search. Retrieval uses a two-tower model: the listing tower is computed offline daily, the query tower in real time; an IVF index with filters returns candidates (a popular place can have around 100,000 available homes, per the 2026 paper). Ranking models score the candidates; Airbnb's 2018 paper describes moving from gradient-boosted trees to neural networks when gains plateaued. Viaduct answers in Ghost Platform form: sections (independent groups of UI components), screens (layout and placements) and actions. Search, listing pages and checkout are built on GP.

Request to book with a timed-out charge (our design on Orpheus's rules). Reservations saves a key and asks orchestration to charge. Pre-RPC records the request and takes a lease; the call to the provider times out, which is retryable. Reservations retries with the same key after the lease expires. Our design then passes the same key downstream or checks the charge status rather than charging blind; the provider reports the charge, post-RPC saves the success, and any later retry gets the saved answer.

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a small booking platform, not Airbnb's:

  • One service and Postgres, with a unique row per listing and night so a night cannot be sold twice.
  • Payments in three phases (record, call, record), with idempotency keys on the primary and leases longer than the call timeout.
  • A hosted payment provider and its own idempotency keys; never hold card numbers.
  • Search with a geographic index and a simple score before any model.
  • A transactional outbox feeding search and email instead of a CDC platform.
  • A few server-described sections for the listing page.

What we would build first

  • One service and Postgres; a unique row per listing and night
  • Payments in three phases, with idempotency keys on the primary
  • A hosted payment provider; never hold card numbers
  • Search in Postgres with a geo index; a simple score before any model
  • An outbox table feeding search and email, not a CDC platform
  • A few server-described sections for the listing page
