Discord chat

How a chat platform pushes every message to every online member, in order: one Elixir process per server, relays and Manifold for fan-out, Rust data services that coalesce reads, and trillions of messages on ScyllaDB.

1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

Discord is group chat and voice for communities. People join servers (called guilds in Discord's code and API) and talk in text channels, voice channels and direct messages, on desktop, mobile and the web. Bots and apps join through a public API.

Everything depends on one idea: each online user holds a long-lived connection, and every event in their servers (messages, typing, presence) is pushed to them in the order it happened. Sending goes over REST; receiving comes down the WebSocket. Making that fan-out cheap enough for servers with a million people online is the story of the architecture.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, from Discord's own pages. The company page reports 90M+ daily active users (Discord internal data, Q4 2025). Older engineering figures are dated: nearly five million concurrent users and millions of events per second (July 2017); more than 2.5 million concurrent voice users (September 2018; the post's body says 2.6 million); trillions of stored messages (March 2023); and the Midjourney server with over ten million members and over a million online at any time (October 2023).

Back of the envelope, from Discord's own illustration: fan-out grows with the square of a server's activity. A thousand people online, each sending one message, means a million notifications; ten thousand means a hundred million.

The functional requirements in the video are ours, written from the product: in-order delivery to everyone permitted to see a message; resuming after a dropped connection; fast history at any age; isolation from giant servers; low-latency voice; and bots that cannot swamp the platform.

3 · Context

Who and what the system talks to (C4 level 1).

Two kinds of client. A member's app sends and fetches over REST and receives live events over a gateway WebSocket; voice goes to separate voice servers over UDP. Push notifications reach closed apps; the push services box is our illustration, not taken from the posts we used.

Bots use the same doors under published rules: 50 requests a second globally plus per-route limits scoped by channel, guild or webhook; 120 gateway events per connection per 60 seconds; and sharding for bots in 2,500+ guilds, with shard_id = (guild_id >> 22) % num_shards. Events without a guild, such as DMs, go to shard 0.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Inside the guild layer, drawn from the 2017 and 2023 posts. The diagram's arrangement is ours; each mechanism is Discord's.

  • Finding the guild. Guilds are placed on nodes with consistent hashing. In 2017 an average user was in five guilds and a VM could hold up to 500,000 sessions; at about 12 µs per inter-process call, a session-node restart spent about 30 seconds on ring lookups. Discord ported the ring to pure Elixir (ex_hash_ring) and stored it as compiled, shared read-only data (mochiglobal, later its own FastGlobal), cutting a lookup to about 0.3 µs and the total to about 750 ms.
  • Backpressure. About five million session processes talked to ten guild registry processes. Timed-out requests piled up and crashed VMs. Discord rejected a circuit breaker (a burst of timeouts would stop all attempts) and used a semaphore built on :ets.update_counter/4, so sessions bail out early when too many requests are outstanding.
  • Manifold. One send/2 cost 30–70 µs of wall-clock time and publishing in a large guild could take 900 ms to 2.1 s. Manifold groups target PIDs by remote node, sends once per node, and a partitioner there splits delivery across cores.
  • Relays and passive sessions (2023). Relays sit between guilds and sessions, each handling up to 15,000 sessions with permission checks. About 90% of user–guild connections in large servers are passive, and fan-out to them is about 90% cheaper; Discord says a 10× cut in work gives roughly 3× the maximum community size. Relays feeding Manifold is our simplification.
  • Workers and ETS. The member list lives in an ETS table, with recent changes on the guild's heap; worker processes use it for member-wide jobs such as an @everyone ping, and to copy gigabytes of members during guild hand-offs without stalling the guild.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

Send a message in a busy server. The app posts to /channels/{id}/messages (content up to 2,000 characters; SEND_MESSAGES permission in guild channels) with a nonce of up to 25 characters. With enforce_nonce, a repeat with the same nonce from the same author “in the past few minutes” returns the existing message instead of creating a new one. The message is stored under its channel, bucket and Snowflake ID and returned. The guild process then orders the MESSAGE_CREATE event and relays deliver it to permitted sessions; the sender sees the event carrying its nonce. The API-to-guild step is our simplification.

Read message history. The app calls Get Channel Messages with one of before, after or around and a limit (default 50, maximum 100). The API calls the data service owning that channel (consistent-hash routing on channel ID), which coalesces identical concurrent requests into one database query. Marking the channel read updates Read States, which is accessed on every connect, send and read; updates schedule a database commit 30 seconds later. Routing the read mark through the API is our simplification.

7 · Data and caching

What is stored where, for how long, and what is never stored.

Messages: partitioned by (channel_id, bucket), clustered by message_id. In 2017 a bucket was a static window of about ten days, chosen to keep partitions under about 100 MB; the 2023 post still describes a static time window and three replicas by default. Message IDs are Snowflakes: 42 bits of milliseconds since the Discord epoch (the start of 2015), then 5-bit worker, 5-bit process and 12-bit increment, so they sort by time.

DataWhereNotes
MessagesScyllaDB, behind Rust data services72 nodes, ~9 TB per node (2023)
Read statesRust service cache (8 million per cache), backed by CassandraCommit 30 s after an update (2020)
Guild member listsETS tables in the guild's Erlang VMIn memory (2023)
Presence, sessionsElixir processesIn memory; our reading of the 2017 post, where presence rebuilt within minutes after a crash

Two lessons from 2017: writing a null in Cassandra creates a tombstone, so Discord stopped writing nulls; and a channel with millions of deleted messages took 20 seconds to load because reads scanned tombstones, which led to shorter tombstone lifespans (10 days down to 2) and tracking empty buckets. Since the 2023 post, the p99 for historical fetches is 15 ms (from 40–125 ms on Cassandra) and for inserts a steady 5 ms (from 5–70 ms).

10 · Patterns used

The named patterns, each linked to its pattern walkthrough.

  • Consistent hashing: guilds to nodes (ex_hash_ring); channel requests to data service instances.
  • Sharding: messages by (channel_id, bucket); bot gateway connections by guild ID.
  • Backpressure: the semaphore on guild registries; less work for passive sessions.
  • Rate limiting: global, per-route and gateway limits, with X-RateLimit-* headers.

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a small community chat app, not Discord's:

  • A WebSocket gateway on an actor runtime (Elixir, Akka or similar): one process per connection, one per server.
  • Messages in Postgres keyed by (channel_id, message_id) with Snowflake-style IDs, and a time bucket planned into the key for the day it moves to a wide-column store.
  • Client nonces for safe retries; sequence numbers and a resume endpoint so dropped connections replay what they missed.
  • Token-bucket limits per user and per route, returning headers and retry_after.
  • An open-source SFU for voice rather than our own.

What we would build first

  • A WebSocket gateway on an actor runtime: a process per connection and per server
  • Postgres messages keyed by (channel, Snowflake-style ID); plan the time bucket now
  • Client nonces for safe retries; sequence numbers so sessions can resume
  • Token-bucket limits per user and per route, with headers
  • An open-source SFU for voice, not our own
