PayPal: moving money safely

How a two-sided wallet moves money safely: balance-first funding, a risk check before money moves, balanced ledger entries, held funds for risky sellers, and Kafka at trillion-message scale.

1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

PayPal describes itself, in its 2025 annual report, as “a global, two-sided network at scale that connects consumers and merchants”. Unlike a card-only processor, it holds an account for both sides of a payment: a consumer can pay a merchant at checkout or send money to a friend, and the money lands in a PayPal balance. A payment can be funded from that balance, a linked bank account, a card and, in some markets, other sources.

Everything in the design depends on one promise: money is never created or lost. Every movement must balance, happen exactly once, and be checked for risk before it moves. Venmo, PayPal's friends-and-family app, is part of the same company but is only context here.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, from PayPal's filings. In 2025 total payment volume (TPV) was $1.79 trillion, up 7%, across 25.4 billion payment transactions, with 439 million active accounts at 31 December 2025 (FY2025 Form 10-K). In the second quarter of 2026 (results published 28 July 2026) TPV was $486.4 billion, up 10%, with 6.8 billion payment transactions and 439 million active accounts. Transaction losses were about $1.3 billion in 2025, 0.07% of TPV.

Back of the envelope: $1.79 trillion ÷ 31.5 million seconds ≈ $57,000 a second, and 25.4 billion ÷ 31.5 million ≈ 800 payments a second on average; peaks are much higher.

The functional requirements in the video are ours, written from the product: every movement balances; retries never send money twice; risk is checked before money moves, in milliseconds; any funding source behind one button; the recipient sees money at once even if funding settles later; and merchants hear about every outcome.

3 · Context

Who and what the system talks to (C4 level 1).

Consumers pay merchants and each other; merchants integrate through the Orders API and receive webhooks. PayPal's help pages say a PayPal payment “will always use any available money in your PayPal balance” first, then a linked bank account, then a linked card; if the preferred method is unavailable, a backup may be used for one-time online payments.

Merchants learn outcomes through webhooks: PayPal retries a non-2xx delivery “up to 25 times over the course of 3 days”, then marks it failed, and merchants should verify each message's signature.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Inside one payment between two people. This whole level is our design; PayPal has not published its payments internals.

  1. Idempotency. PayPal's REST APIs take a PayPal-Request-Id header on POST calls; a repeat returns the latest status of the first request rather than repeating it, for as long as the server stores the id. We use the same rule internally.
  2. Funding plan. Balance first, then bank, then card, as PayPal's help pages describe. Alice sends Bob £20 with £5 in her balance: £5 from balance, £15 from her card.
  3. Risk gate. Allow, step up, or decline, before anything is written, so a decline needs no undo.
  4. Postings. Debit Alice's balance £5, debit card clearing £15, credit Bob's balance £20. All entries and an outbox row commit in one database transaction.
  5. Settlement. The card is captured; when funds arrive, card clearing returns to zero. Reversals are new entries; old entries are never edited. Clearing accounts are reconciled daily against bank and processor reports.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

Send money (our design). App → payments service with a request id; risk score; card authorisation for the shortfall; balanced postings; “sent” to Alice and “received” to Bob; card settles later and clearing is matched.

Checkout with PayPal (documented). The buyer clicks the PayPal button; the merchant's backend creates an order (intent CAPTURE or AUTHORIZE) and gets an order id; the buyer completes checkout in the PayPal pop-up; the page receives onApprove; the merchant's backend calls capture “to move the money”. Sending PayPal-Request-Id makes the capture safe to retry. Where risk scoring sits in this flow is our placement, not documented. A webhook follows, retried for up to three days.

10 · Patterns used

The named patterns, each linked to its pattern walkthrough.

  • Idempotency: PayPal-Request-Id on POST calls.
  • Transactional outbox: ledger entries and their events commit together (our design).
  • Saga: authorise, post, settle across systems, with compensating entries instead of rollback (our design).
  • Event streaming: Kafka at about a trillion messages a day.
  • Retry with backoff: webhook delivery for up to three days.

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a small wallet, not PayPal's:

  • One service and Postgres, with a double-entry ledger table from day one and a check that every transaction sums to zero.
  • Request ids with a unique constraint on (account, id); replay the first result.
  • Balance-first funding, with one card processor for the rest.
  • A rules-based risk check in the payment path; add models when there is data.
  • An outbox feeding a queue for notifications and signed webhooks, retried with backoff.
  • Daily reconciliation of clearing accounts against bank and processor reports.

What we would build first

  • One service and Postgres, with a double-entry ledger from day one
  • Request ids with a unique constraint; replay the first result
  • Balance-first funding; one card processor for the rest
  • A rules-based risk check in the path; models later
  • Outbox to a queue for notifications and webhooks
  • Daily reconciliation against bank and processor reports
