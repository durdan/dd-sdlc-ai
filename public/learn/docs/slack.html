Slack: messages in real time

How a work chat delivers every message worldwide in about half a second: channel servers on a consistent hash ring, gateways at the edge, MySQL resharded with Vitess, Kafka in front of the job queue, and availability zones that can be drained in minutes.

1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

Slack is messaging for work. People in a workspace talk in channels, direct messages and threads, on desktop, mobile and the web; apps and bots join in through the Web API. Salesforce acquired Slack on 21 July 2021.

Everything depends on one idea: the channel is the unit of everything. Messages, reactions, bookmarks and channel joins are all events on a channel ID (and IDs for users, teams, files and huddles are handled the same way). Each channel is owned by one in-memory channel server, and every online client subscribed to it receives its events over a single WebSocket.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, from Slack's own statements. Slack last published daily active users in October 2019: more than 12 million in September 2019, with more than six million paid seats. Its last results before the acquisition (quarter ended 30 April 2021, published 3 June 2021) counted over 169,000 paid customers; slack.com now says 200K+ paid customers (undated page, checked October 2026).

Engineering figures, each dated: 2.3 million database queries a second at peak (December 2020); over 1.4 billion jobs on the busiest days at a peak of 33,000 a second (December 2017); messages delivered worldwide in 500 ms, with about 16 million channels served per channel-server host at peak (April 2023). Slack cites a 99.99% availability SLA, under an hour of downtime a year (August 2023).

Back of the envelope: 33,000 jobs a second at peak against 1.4 billion a day (about 16,000 a second on average) means peaks around twice the average, and the real-time post notes extra spikes at the top of every hour from reminders and scheduled messages.

What the system must do (our list)

  • A message reaches every online member of the channel in well under a second
  • Opening the app is fast, even in a workspace of tens of thousands
  • Reactions, edits and joins arrive the same way as messages
  • Slow work, like notifications, never slows the send
  • Losing a server, or a whole data centre, is barely noticed
  • 99.99% availability, under an hour of downtime a year

3 · Context

Who and what the system talks to (C4 level 1).

Members' clients call the HTTPS API to boot, send and read, and hold one WebSocket for live events. Apps and bots use the same Web API and receive events. Slack runs on Amazon Web Services; two of the incidents below began in the cloud network. 5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Inside real-time messaging, from the April 2023 post; the arrangement on screen is our drawing.

  • Channel servers are mapped to channels by consistent hashing of the channel ID. At peak about 16 million channels are served per host.
  • CHARMs (consistent hash ring managers) define each ring in Consul and replace unhealthy channel servers; a new one is ready in under 20 seconds, and users of its channels see higher latency for less than 20 seconds.
  • Admin servers read the current ring from Consul and route each message to its channel server.
  • Gateway servers run in several regions. A channel server sends each message to every gateway subscribed to the channel, and each gateway to every subscribed client. A failed gateway region is drained to the nearest healthy one.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

Boot and connect. The client fetches its user token and WebSocket setup information from the Webapp, then opens a WebSocket to the nearest edge region, where Envoy forwards it to a gateway server. The gateway fetches the user's information and channel list from the Webapp, sends the client its first message, then subscribes to the relevant channel servers asynchronously.

Send a message. The client sends the message to the Webapp API. The Webapp sends it to an admin server, which uses the channel ID and the hash ring to find the channel server; the channel server sends it to every subscribed gateway worldwide, and each gateway to every subscribed client. The store-then-publish order and the job enqueue step in our sequence are our assumption; Slack's job queue post does say the queue runs for every message post. Transient events such as typing indicators go client → gateway → channel server → gateways → clients and are not persisted.

Slack started on MySQL sharded by workspace, each shard a pair of servers in different data centres replicating both ways. Large enterprise customers became hot spots and a shard outage took down every workspace on it. The Vitess migration began in 2017; by December 2020 Vitess served 99% of queries, 2.3 million a second at peak (about 2M reads, 300K writes), with 2 ms median and 11 ms p99 latency. Message data was sharded by channel ID. Vitess also underpins data residency, with clusters in six regions.

On the client, the 2019 desktop rewrite caches app files and a copy of the Redux store, cutting boot time by about 50% and allowing offline reading of conversations already opened.

10 · Patterns used

The named patterns, each linked to its pattern walkthrough.

  • Consistent hashing: channels to channel servers, users to presence servers, teams to Flannel instances.
  • Publish-subscribe: gateways subscribe to channel servers; clients to what is on screen.
  • Sharding: Vitess keyspaces by channel, user or team.
  • Competing consumers: workers draining the job queue.
  • Cell-based architecture: availability zones as drainable silos.
  • Cache-aside and graceful degradation: Memcached in front of Vitess; throttled boot in 2022.

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a small team chat product, not Slack's:

  • Send over HTTP; push events over one WebSocket per client, with a gateway tier that only knows subscriptions.
  • Postgres, with messages keyed by channel ID from day one so they can be sharded later.
  • One in-memory owner per channel, placed by consistent hashing, with fast replacement.
  • A durable queue (not Redis alone) for notifications, unfurls and reminders.
  • Clients subscribe only to what is on screen, and cache their own state for fast boot.
  • A boot throttle, so a backend problem slows new logins instead of breaking connected users.

What we would build first

  • Send over HTTP; push events over one WebSocket per client
  • Postgres, with messages keyed by channel ID from day one
  • One in-memory process per channel, placed by consistent hashing
  • A durable queue for notifications and unfurls, not Redis alone
  • Clients subscribe only to what is on screen; cache state locally
  • Throttle boot when the backend struggles; keep connected users working
