Shopify: surviving Black Friday

How millions of shops survive the busiest weekend of the year: a modular Rails monolith on isolated pods of shops, live shop moves with Ghostferry, a fair checkout queue at the edge, Semian circuit breakers and months of load tests.

1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

Shopify runs millions of online shops on one platform. Buyers browse storefronts and check out; merchants run their shops from the admin; app developers extend shops through APIs. Most of the year the load is predictable. Black Friday–Cyber Monday (BFCM) is not: traffic rises within minutes, and individual merchants run flash sales on top.

Everything in the design depends on one idea: isolation. Shops are split into pods, each with its own datastores, so a flash sale or a broken database only ever affects the shops in one pod.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, from Shopify's release of 2 December 2025 (figures approximate and unaudited). Over BFCM 2025, "from early Friday in New Zealand to late Monday in California", merchants sold $14.6 billion, up 27% (24% at constant currency), peaking at $5.1 million a minute at 12:01 pm EST on Black Friday, with an average cart of $114.70 and 81+ million buyers. At peak the platform served 489 million requests a minute at the edge, more than 117 million a minute on app servers and 31.8 million API requests a minute. Over the weekend: 2.2 trillion edge requests, 14.8 trillion database queries, 1.75 trillion database writes and 90 PB of data.

Back of the envelope (our arithmetic): 489M ÷ 60 ≈ 8 million edge requests a second; 117M ÷ 60 ≈ 2 million app-server requests a second, so about four edge requests for each one reaching the app servers; $5.1M ÷ $114.70 ≈ 44,000 orders a minute at peak if every cart were average.

The functional requirements in the video are ours, written from what Shopify has described: contain each shop's spikes, keep checkout working when optional parts fail, queue buyers fairly, move shops without downtime, prove capacity in advance, and keep a very large codebase safe to change.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Pods. Shopify's 2018 post explains that by 2015 it could no longer buy a larger database server and had to shard; in 2016 it reorganised its runtime into pods. "A pod consists of a set of shops that live on a fully isolated set of datastores": its own MySQL, Redis and Memcached. Job workers, app servers and load balancers are shared, but "all shared resources can only ever communicate to a single pod at a time"; every unit of work, web request or job, is assigned to one pod. In August 2018 Shopify had more than a hundred pods.

  • Sorting Hat, in the load balancers, matches each request to a pod using a list of rules and adds a header; app servers use the header to connect to the right datastores. The 2021 shard-balancing post says the load balancer forwards each request to the pod for its shop using a routing table kept in a separate, unsharded database.
  • Disaster recovery. Each pod is assigned a pair of data centres, one active and one recovery site. Pod Mover moves a pod to its recovery data centre "in a minute without dropping requests or jobs" (2018).
  • Why it matters. In February 2016 a celebrity cosmetics flash sale took down that store and others on the same database shard (2017 post). Pods make the blast radius one pod.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

Moving a shop between pods (shard balancing, September 2021). Shop sizes vary and busy shops can cluster on one pod; Shopify's example has two merchants running flash sales on the same day, and a near 4× spread between the busiest and quietest shards. Ghostferry, Shopify's open-source tool for copying data between MySQL servers "with minimal amount of downtime", moves one shop at a time:

  1. Batch copy and binlog tailing. Rows are copied in batches with SELECT … FOR UPDATE; meanwhile the source binlog is tailed and only that shop's changes are replayed. Traffic is still served from the source.
  2. Cut-over. Every unit of work scoped to the shop holds the reader side of a Redis-backed multi-reader-single-writer lock. The mover takes the writer side; Ghostferry replays up to the final binlog position. Shopify gives no measured downtime, only that cut-over "is designed to be a short process".
  3. Switch, verify, prune. The routing table entry is updated and the lock released; verifiers check completeness and corruption; stale data on the old pod is pruned later. Ghostferry's core algorithm has a TLA+ model.

Checkout during a flash sale (February 2017). The edge throttles checkout with a leaky bucket built into the Lua load balancers. A plain 429 would read as a crashed site, so excess buyers get a queue page, rendered by Shopify and cached in Nginx, that polls. The first version admitted pollers effectively at random, and some waited up to 40 minutes. Part II made it fair: the first checkout attempt is timestamped in a signed cookie, and each load balancer computes its own "now serving" threshold with a proportional controller based on spare bucket capacity, keeping the edge stateless and avoiding a data store that "would be a new point of failure". Once admitted, the order is created in the shop's pod and payment is authorised; Shopify's payments team (2022) uses an idempotency key per payment attempt and Semian circuit breakers on providers. 9 · Failure modes

What breaks, what users see, and what protects them.

FailureWhat protects buyersBasis

Database, Redis or provider slowsSemian circuit breaker opens; calls fail fast. Bulkheads cap workers per resource. For card payments, the merchant's country is part of the breaker identifierDocumented

For BFCM 2025, Shopify ran five scale tests from April to October with Genghis; test 4 reached 146 million requests a minute and 80,000+ checkouts a minute (and triggered an unplanned failover), and a final test exercised p99 traffic at 200M RPM. The programme simulated 150% of the previous year's load. Shopify's 2020 post on Semian shows why tuning matters: in a worked example, changing two parameters cut the extra worker utilisation needed during an outage from 263% to 4%.

10 · Patterns used

The named patterns, each linked to its pattern walkthrough.

  • Cell-based architecture: pods of shops, each with its own datastores.
  • Sharding: MySQL split by shop, with live shop moves.
  • Rate limiting: a leaky bucket and a fair queue for checkout.
  • Circuit breaker and bulkhead: Semian on every remote resource.
  • Graceful degradation: guest checkout without the session store.
  • Read replicas: storefront reads kept off the primary.
  • Strangler fig: the Storefront Renderer was verified against the old code on mirrored traffic, then given traffic gradually.

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a multi-tenant shop platform, not Shopify's:

  • One modular application; enforce boundaries in CI from day one.
  • A tenant ID on every table, even with one database.
  • A tenant-to-database routing table, even with one entry, so pods can come later without a rewrite.
  • Timeouts and circuit breakers on every remote call.
  • A waiting room at the edge for flash sales.
  • Load tests at 1.5× last year's peak, months ahead.
