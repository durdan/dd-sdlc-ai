Amazon: the shopping cart that never says no

Why adding to Amazon's cart never fails: services owned by small teams, Dynamo's hash ring, sloppy quorums and vector clocks, and what changed with DynamoDB and cells.

1 · Overview

What it is, who uses it, and the one idea everything depends on.

Amazon's retail site looks like one website, but since the early 2000s it has been built from many services, each owned by a small team. The 2007 Dynamo paper says a single page request makes the rendering engine call over 150 services.

This case study follows one promise: adding to the cart must never fail. Dynamo, Amazon's key-value store described at SOSP in October 2007, was built for exactly that. It is "always writeable": it accepts a write even when replicas are down or the network is split, and leaves the application to reconcile divergent versions later. The cart is the paper's main example.

2 · Requirements and scale

What users need, the quality targets, and back-of-envelope numbers.

Scale, dated. The Dynamo paper (2007) reports "well over 3 million checkouts in a single day", "hundreds of thousands of concurrently active sessions", "tens of millions customers at peak times" and "tens of thousands of servers located in many data centers around the world". A typical Dynamo SLA was that 99.9% of reads and writes complete within 300 ms.

Today's figures are AWS's, and cover all of Amazon, not the cart alone. For Prime Day 2026 (23–26 June 2026), AWS reports DynamoDB processed over 59 trillion requests and peaked at 192 million requests per second, serving properties including the Amazon.com sites, Alexa and all Amazon fulfilment centres (AWS News Blog, 6 October 2026).

Requirements. From the paper: the cart accepts adds and removes amid network and server failures; latency is judged at the 99.9th percentile rather than the mean; the store survives the loss of whole data centres; capacity grows a node at a time. Ours: checkout is exact about price, stock and payment; peak days are routine.

3 · Context

Who and what the system talks to (C4 level 1).

Two kinds of person use the site: shoppers (web and app) and sellers, whose side we simplify into one box. Outside parties: AWS, which the site runs on (AWS says Graviton powered up to 49% of the EC2 compute Amazon.com used on Prime Day 2026); payment networks and banks (illustrative; little is published); and fulfilment centres and carriers, which ship orders.

The history behind it: the Distributed Computing Manifesto (May 1998, published by Werner Vogels in 2022) proposed moving off a "two-tier, client-server architecture" that was "essentially data bound" to services that reach data only through interfaces. AWS describes the teams as small enough to be fed with two pizzas.

5 · Deep dive

Inside the most interesting component (C4 level 3).

Inside Dynamo, from the 2007 paper. Every node is equal; the paper calls it "a completely decentralized system".

  • Partitioning. Consistent hashing: the hash output is a ring; a key belongs to the first node clockwise, and the following nodes hold replicas. Virtual nodes give each machine many positions, spreading load and letting bigger machines take more.
  • Replication. Each key is stored on N nodes from its preference list, which holds more than N nodes so failures can be skipped. The common (N, R, W) setting is (3, 2, 2). A put succeeds once W−1 other nodes respond to the coordinator; a get waits for R and returns every causally unrelated version.
  • Sloppy quorum and hinted handoff. Operations go to the first N healthy nodes. A copy meant for a down node goes elsewhere with a hint, and is handed back on recovery.
  • Versions. Vector clocks, lists of (node, counter) pairs, track causality. Past a threshold (the paper says "say 10"), the oldest pair is dropped.
  • Membership and repair. Gossip spreads membership changes (each node contacts a random peer every second). Read repair fixes stale replicas after a read; Merkle-tree anti-entropy fixes longer drift.
  • Storage. Pluggable local engines: Berkeley DB Transactional Data Store, BDB Java Edition, MySQL.

6 · Core flows

Two or three requests that matter, step by step.

Add to cart while a replica is down. The cart service sends a put with the cart and the context from its last read. The coordinator writes locally with a new vector clock, replicates to the next nodes, skips the unreachable one, and sends that copy to the next healthy node with a hint. Once W = 2 copies exist, the shopper sees success. The hinted copy is handed back later. The paper also measured client-driven coordination: 99.9th percentile read latency fell from 68.9 ms to 30.4 ms.

Read a diverged cart. The coordinator waits for R = 2 replies. If the versions are concurrent (neither clock descends from the other) it returns both. The cart service merges them and writes the result back with a clock that supersedes both; stale replicas are updated (read repair). Merging means "an 'add to cart' operation is never lost", but "deleted items can resurface".

How often carts diverge: over 24 hours, 99.94% of requests saw exactly one version; 0.00057% saw two, 0.00047% three and 0.00009% four. The paper attributes most divergence to concurrent writers, "usually triggered by busy robots", not to failures.

Dynamo is not DynamoDB. The 2022 USENIX ATC paper says Dynamo was created for shopping-cart data, but was single-tenant and self-managed, and its operational complexity became a barrier. DynamoDB (2012) combines Dynamo's incremental scalability and predictable performance with SimpleDB's managed experience. Tables split into partitions holding contiguous key ranges, with replicas across Availability Zones; each replication group uses Multi-Paxos for leader election; only the leader serves writes and strongly consistent reads, while any replica serves eventually consistent reads. Request routers look up partitions in a metadata service (cached, with a hit rate of about 99.75%, later backed by an in-memory store called MemDS). Global admission control uses token buckets.

10 · Patterns used

The named patterns, each linked to its pattern walkthrough.

  • Consistent hashing with virtual nodes: Dynamo's ring.
  • Sharding by key, in Dynamo and DynamoDB.
  • Cell-based architecture and shuffle sharding: limit the blast radius.
  • Timeouts chosen from a latency percentile (the Builders' Library uses p99.9 for a 0.1% false-timeout rate).
  • Retries with backoff and jitter, at a single layer of the stack.
  • Rate limiting: DynamoDB's token buckets and global admission control.

12 · What we would build first

An MVP cut and our recommendations.

Our recommendations for a cart today, not Amazon's:

  • One cart service on a managed key-value store, across availability zones from day one.
  • Cart items keyed by product, updated with conditional writes on a version number; no vector clocks.
  • Treat the cart as a wish list: re-check price and stock at checkout.
  • An idempotency key on place-order, so a retried checkout charges once.
  • Timeouts from measured percentiles; retries with jitter at one layer only.
  • Cells once a bad deploy reaching every customer becomes the main risk.

What we would build first

  • One cart service on a managed key-value store, across zones
  • Cart items keyed by product; conditional writes, not vector clocks
  • The cart is a wish list: re-check price and stock at checkout
  • An idempotency key on place-order
  • Timeouts, and retries with jitter at one layer
  • Cells once blast radius matters
